# AI tool usage

I used one AI tool for this exercise: Codex.

My workspace had two repositories in it: the original take-home repository Rahul shared, and a separate repository for my submission. I used Codex across that workspace, so it could refer back to the field notes, schema, model, and the files I was producing without me repeatedly copying everything into a chat.

I started by using Codex as a domain tutor. I asked it to explain the business at a basic level: what Harbor does, what occupancy and in-place rent mean, and why units, leases, jobs, and invoices are different operating objects. I then read the field notes and inspected the supplied data myself. After that pass, I gave Codex my own notes and asked it to help organize them into a taxonomy of nouns, verbs, and metric classes. It pointed out a few nouns I had missed and challenged me on some of the A/B/C/D classifications. The final classifications reflect that discussion; I did not force a disagreement where its reasoning matched mine.

For the model, I opened a fresh conversation and supplied only the taxonomy as context. Before asking for any YAML, I described how I thought the data model should work: two occupancy definitions behind one business term, rent protected at unit grain, open jobs as a complete predicate, and tenant-specific meaning selected through a profile instead of runtime `if tenant` logic. Codex helped turn that into a consistent `model.yaml`. The area where I relied on it most heavily was relationship cardinality. I was less confident about expressing one-to-many and many-to-one relationships correctly, so Codex did much more of that translation than I did.

`contract.md` came from another back-and-forth. My view was that the contract should be the governing layer between a business request and execution, rather than a description of happy-path SQL. The product judgments I brought to that discussion were that tenant scope must come from an authenticated Principal, a request must not be allowed to choose its tenant, a denied field must be refused rather than returned as `null` or zero, and an unsafe grain must fail rather than be patched with an unproven `DISTINCT`. I also chose not to invent fallbacks: if Northline's official snapshot is missing, or the registered occupancy-history query is unavailable, the resolver should say so instead of substituting a convenient answer. Codex helped me structure these decisions into a clearer contract and a consistent set of error classes.

There were also judgments that came directly from my reading of the notes. I treated the hardcoded five-day overdue rule as a tenant-specific, time-boxed exception rather than a platform primitive. I rejected the request to email a VP and draft an owner note after a 2% occupancy move because approval, recipients, duplicate suppression, failures, and audit behavior were undefined. I kept Cedar's “not yet invoiced” jobs separate from Harbor's open-jobs definition instead of collapsing two different business rules under one label.

I did **not** use Codex to derive the proof SQL from the schema dump or to act as a stand-in resolver. I worked out the intended queries and checked the data myself. I did use Codex for debugging when a query failed, especially for table, column, entity, and join-reference mistakes. That was a practical use of its workspace context: it could usually spot a naming mismatch faster than I could retrace several files. The business definition, expected grain, filters, and decision to refuse unsafe cases were not delegated to it.

Finally, once the model, contract, and SQL proofs were in place, I gave Codex the query results and screenshots I had collected and asked it to help organize the proof files and overall submission in the requested structure. I also used it for prose editing and consistency checks across the README, taxonomy, contract, model, proofs, and memo. I recorded the same workflow in the Loom rather than presenting the submission as AI-free work.
